When businesses buy technology, it's tempting to choose the most powerful option available. More memory, a faster processor, the latest model — it feels like a safe, future-proof decision. In practice, it often isn't.

## Power you don't use is money you don't get back

A receptionist managing appointments, an accounts officer working in spreadsheets and a designer editing video all need very different machines. Buying top-end equipment for every role means paying for capacity most people will never use. That budget could have gone toward something that makes a real difference — reliable networking, power backup, or the software that ties everything together.

## The bottleneck is often somewhere else

A fast computer won't help if:

- the **internet connection** is unreliable,
- the **network** in the office is poorly set up,
- there's **no backup power**, so work stops during outages,
- or the **software** your team uses is slow, disconnected or badly configured.

Technology works as an environment. Upgrading one part while ignoring the rest rarely delivers the improvement people expect.

## Compatibility matters more than specification

Will the new equipment run the software your business depends on? Will it work with your printers, scanners and point-of-sale devices? Can it be maintained and repaired locally? Can parts be sourced when something fails? These questions matter more to daily operations than benchmark scores.

## Think about the total cost

The purchase price is only part of the cost. Consider:

- **Power consumption,** especially where electricity is expensive or unreliable.
- **Maintenance and support** over the life of the equipment.
- **Lifespan** — when will it need replacing, and what will that cost?
- **Standardisation** — a consistent set of devices is easier and cheaper to support than a mix of models.

## A better approach

Good technology procurement starts with questions, not catalogues:

1. What work does each person or role actually do?
2. Which software must the equipment run, now and in the near future?
3. What does the environment look like — power, connectivity, space?
4. What's the budget across purchase, running and support costs?

From there, you can match equipment to real requirements and spend where it matters.
