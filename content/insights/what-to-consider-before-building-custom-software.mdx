Custom software can give a business a real advantage: technology that fits how it operates, connects its processes and grows with it. But a successful project depends on decisions made long before the first line of code. Here's what to consider.

## Be clear about the problem, not just the solution

"We need an app" is a solution. "Our sales team can't see current stock levels when they're with customers" is a problem. Starting with the problem keeps the project focused on outcomes, and often reveals a simpler or better solution than the one first imagined.

## Know who will use it

List the people who'll use the system — staff, managers, customers, partners — and what each needs to do. A system that works for the manager but frustrates the people entering data every day will struggle. The best requirements come from talking to real users.

## Decide what matters most

Not every feature needs to be in the first version. Separate what's **essential** from what's **nice to have**. Launching a focused first version sooner lets you learn from real use and invest in the right improvements next.

## Think about what it needs to connect to

Does the system need to work with accounting software, mobile-money payments, SMS or email notifications, existing databases or hardware such as printers and scanners? Integration needs affect cost and design, so identify them early.

## Plan for the environment it will run in

Where will people use it — in an office, on the road, on a farm? On computers, tablets or phones? With reliable internet or intermittent connectivity? The answers shape the technology choices and, sometimes, the equipment you'll need.

## Budget for the whole lifecycle

Software isn't finished at launch. Plan for hosting, support, maintenance, security updates and future improvements. A realistic budget covers the system's life, not just its build.

## Own your data and your system

Agree clearly who owns the source code and the data, where data is stored, how it's backed up and how it's protected. These questions are easier to settle at the start than later.

## Choose a partner who asks questions

The right development partner should want to understand your business before proposing technology. If a provider jumps straight to features and prices without asking how you operate, be cautious.
